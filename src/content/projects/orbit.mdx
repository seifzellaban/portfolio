## The brief

Orbit started as a 2024 NASA Space Apps Challenge build: make the solar system explorable in a browser, and make near-Earth objects feel less abstract.

Space data is usually presented as tables, charts, or flat diagrams. That works for researchers, but it hides the thing that makes the topic compelling — scale, motion, and the fact that these objects are actually moving around us.

## What Orbit does

Orbit renders the solar system as an interactive 3D scene. You can move through it, look at planets from different angles, and see how near-Earth objects relate to the orbits around them.

The scene is driven by real data rather than hand-placed props. Planet positions and NEO information come from NASA's public APIs, so the visualization reflects the actual state of the system instead of a static illustration.

## How it is built

The frontend is Next.js with Three.js doing the rendering. Planetary textures and models were prepared in Blender, then exported for the web. The challenge was keeping the scene readable and performant on ordinary laptops, not just machines with a dedicated GPU.

A few constraints shaped the build:

- **Level of detail** — distant objects use simpler geometry and textures.
- **Lazy loading** — heavy assets load as they come into view.
- **Data boundaries** — API responses are normalized before they reach the scene graph.

## What I learned

Web 3D is mostly a performance discipline. The rendering code is the easy part; the hard part is deciding what the user does not need to see at any given moment.

The project also made me much more comfortable with Blender as part of a web pipeline — not as a separate art step, but as a source of assets that have to survive compression, loading, and mobile screens.

## Reflections

Orbit is the project that made orbital mechanics click for me. Once you watch a near-Earth object move through a scene you built, the data stops being abstract. That is the whole argument for interactive visualization.
