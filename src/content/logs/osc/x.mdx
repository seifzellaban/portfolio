# Fired for Doing the Job

Most managers fire people for theft or skipping work. OSC fired me for overproductivity.

That sounds like a LinkedIn humble-brag. It happened. As Head of HR, I built systems to make the department run. The board preferred noise.

---

## Chapter One: Schrödinger's KPI

I walked in as HR head thinking organization mattered. Over several weeks, I built a tracking system in Google Drive and trained my team on it. My members enjoyed the work.

The board asked: **"Where are your tasks on Discord?"**

I had color-coded sheets sitting in a shared Drive folder. To the board, a task outside a Discord channel full of memes and `@everyone` pings did not exist.

**The correct move:** Focus on performance theater. Post in Discord four times an hour to announce you opened a PDF. Tag the whole server after bolding row 4, column C. In that room, a finished job without a broadcast counts as unauthorized work.

---

## Chapter Two: The Audacity of Solutions

My next offense was fixing a broken workflow.

The tech committee had ignored our requests for months. We needed a tool. Without an official coding title, I stayed up through the night and shipped the script.

I expected a thank-you. The board replied: **"Who gave you permission?"**

**The correct move:** Schedule a pre-meeting to debate holding a meeting. Draft a request form and wait two months for the tech lead to reject it. Let the department stall after that. Patching a leak insults the people staring at the rising water.

---

## Chapter Three: The Vampire Shift

The board pinged me at 11:00 PM: *"Hey, need this done. Deadline is tomorrow morning."*

I asked if they could assign tasks during daylight hours. They answered: **"Stop delaying tasks."**

**The correct move:** Thank them for the midnight shift. Cancel your plans and turn in a rushed draft at 4:00 AM. They will leave it unread until noon.

---

## Chapter Four: Support

Boards exist to back up committee heads. Ours interrupted my sessions to question my methods in front of my team. They called my ideas "unaligned," their polite term for anything they did not invent.

My 17 members saw through the theater. They enjoyed their work and ignored the board's attempts to turn them against me.

**The correct move:** Be bad at the job. A confused head gives the board a chance to lecture and feel useful. They hated looking unprepared next to a working committee.

---

## Chapter Five: The 93 Missing Minutes

Our summer training ran **100 minutes late**. The board looked for someone to blame and picked HR: **"The event ran late because HR was slow with the attendee entry."**

Check the record. My 12-person team stood at the registration line at **11:59 AM**, one minute before doors opened. Nine members scanned codes at the entrance; three managed seating inside. We waited on the venue to let anyone in.

Our database logs recorded the final attendee entering **7 minutes late**. He arrived 7 minutes late.

Subtract 7 from 100 and you get **93 minutes**.

The tech team burned those 93 minutes. They fought a dead microphone for an hour. After that, their Linux presentation crashed mid-session because they did not know Linux, forcing a reboot into Windows while the audience watched.

**The correct move:** Apologize for failing to repair a microphone from the registration desk. Teach the tech committee Linux before the event starts.

---

## Chapter Six: Four Seconds Flat

They called to let me go and offered a parting gift: **"You can say goodbye to your members before you leave. We're generous like that."**

I did not argue. I asked them to remove me from the group chats. An organization that needed months to approve a basic tool banned me from the Discord server in under four seconds.

**The correct move:** Admire the turnaround time. That same urgency would have saved the summer training.

---

## The Verdict

They said I failed to stick to my role. They had a point. I ran HR and covered for tech while my team dealt with board politics.

To survive in a place like that, skip building and post a daily GIF in Discord. My nights stay quiet without 11:00 PM pings, and my team knows the truth.

***

## Update: Welcome Day

After OSC removed me, several of us moved to MSP and rebuilt it. On Welcome Day, freshmen packed our booth.

OSC's booth struggled. Their PR team included new members I had planned to train before my exit. They walked over and asked: *"How do you do it? How do you retain these circles and keep everyone engaged?"*

I offered a 50/50 split on the next ushering wave so they could watch and pick up the ropes.

Their new President walked over and stopped me: **"Can you just not help our members?"**

I smiled and said okay. Saving my energy beat offering free labor.

The next day, one of their members ran up asking for help. I raised my voice so people around us could hear: **"NAHHH. Your President told me not to help you. Go ask her."**

That afternoon, a friend from their side came over needing power. We had a splitter; they had none. He reached for an outlet and I blocked the plug: **"Take off that ID badge."**

He stared at me. I explained that if his board spotted him using my power strip in an OSC badge, they would spin a week of drama out of it. He slipped the badge off and plugged in.

---

## Update 2: The Spin Machine

OSC leadership lost their team and botched their tech, yet refused to look at their own mistakes. They kept trash-talking us, painting us as villains who walked out.

You cannot fix a broken microphone with gossip, and you cannot run a Linux terminal without knowing the commands. We have power strips and registration lines that open on time.

---

## Update 3: Resonance at MSP

I head the **Resonance** committee at MSP, building without manufactured drama.

My board gives constructive feedback. If I make a mistake, they help me fix it rather than plot an exit. Looking at my members, I see future leaders who will take over after me.

We have real work to build, aiming to reshape AI at this university, **Insha'Allah**.

Thank you to OSC for firing me. You did me a favor.
