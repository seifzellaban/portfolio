My season as committee head is wrapping up, so I wrote down the exact process for putting together a Head Plan that holds up in review and through the semester.

*reading time: 8 minutes*
***

## Why the Head Plan Matters

A Head Plan lays out your goals, milestones, and measurable checkpoints for the season. Without one, you guess from week to week and scramble as soon as midterms hit.

Good plans account for disruptions ahead of time. Think through your committee's real bottlenecks, set timelines that survive exam weeks, and write down fallback steps before problems show up.

## Know the Expectations First

Ask your VP, President, and the OSC board for their criteria before opening a blank doc. Guessing wastes weeks.

### Check the requirements
Requirements shift between seasons. Confirm with your VP and board:
  * The rubric the board uses to grade your plan.
  * Required sections (events, goals, timelines, and budget).

### Learn the review process
  * Identify who reviews your plan and what they score for (feasibility, clarity, and fit with OSC's season goals).
  * Ask how they deliver feedback so you can revise fast.

### Read past seasons
  * Pull last year's plan for your committee.
  * Ask previous heads what worked, what broke, and what they would cut.

## Core Sections of a Strong Plan

Include each of these sections in your final document.

### Committee Vision & Mission
State why your committee exists this season, what you intend to ship by handover, and what members will learn. Connect your objectives to OSC's broader season plan.

### Goals & KPIs
Write SMART goals (Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, Time-bound). Pair each goal with a quantifiable KPI you can review on a fixed schedule.

### Key Activities & Events
List the workshops, projects, and campaigns you plan to run. For each item, define concrete deliverables so members know what done looks like.

### Team Structure & Roles
Map out your hierarchy, reporting lines, and decision owners. Clear roles stop duplicated work and keep handoffs clean.

### Timeline & Execution Plan
Break the season into monthly or weekly blocks with milestone dates. A visible schedule keeps the team focused once classes get heavy.

### Resource & Budget Needs
List required people, tools, venues, and materials alongside realistic cost estimates and funding sources. Clear numbers help leadership approve your budget faster.

### Risk Assessment & Contingency Plans
Name the risks that could derail your season (speaker cancellations, venue issues, or mid-season dropouts) and write a specific fallback for each one.

## Homework Before You Draft

Research separates a serious plan from generic filler.

### Audit committee history
Read past event reports and budget sheets. Spot recurring issues so you fix old failures rather than repeat them.

### Talk to your team
Run quick surveys and one-on-one chats with current and prospective members. Ground your targets in their real capacity and skills.

### Coordinate with other committees
Sync with fellow heads early to avoid clashing dates and competition for venues or audiences. Pitch joint events where two committees can split the workload.

### Check the OSC master calendar
Mark freeze dates, event windows, and university exam weeks on your timeline first. Schedule prep time around academic deadlines so attendance does not collapse.

## Writing for the Board

Keep sentences short and cut buzzwords. Structure the doc for fast scanning with clear headings, bullet points, budget tables, and timeline charts. Tie each planned activity back to a measurable goal.

Visual aids carry weight in review. Use tables for budgets and Gantt charts for project schedules. Keep the tone direct and human rather than stiff.

## Common Traps

* **Vague promises:** Writing "We will host events" without dates, formats, or goals forces your VP to guess and costs you points.
* **Overpromising:** Scheduling ten big projects for a small team with no budget burns members out by mid-season.
* **Skipping backups:** Power outages, canceled speakers, and shrinking headcount happen. Plans without fallbacks collapse on contact.
* **Recycling last year's doc:** Treat past plans as reference material, not copy-paste templates.
* **Ignoring exams:** Booking major sessions during midterms or finals guarantees empty seats.
* **Unmeasurable KPIs:** "Increase engagement" means nothing. "Raise average workshop attendance by 20% over last semester" gives you a target you can check.
* **Clashing with the master calendar:** Competing against another campus event kills turnout.
* **Overloading new recruits:** Handing high-pressure deliverables to untrained members drives them out.
* **No post-event review:** Without feedback forms and debriefs, you repeat the same mistakes next event.

## Review and Submission

Send an early draft to a former committee head or trusted peer for blunt feedback on feasibility and clarity. Follow all formatting rules and submit ahead of the deadline.

Prepare for the review interview by knowing the reasoning behind your budget, dates, and fallback options so you can defend each choice under questioning.

## Pre-Submission Checklist

* Vision aligns with OSC's season goals.
* Goals are measurable and realistic.
* Activities include concrete dates.
* Budget is accurate and justified.
* Risks have specific fallback plans.
* Dates do not clash with other committees or exams.

## After Approval

Treat the Head Plan as a working document, not a one-time checkbox. Revise dates, scope, and tactics as your team grows and circumstances change through the season.
