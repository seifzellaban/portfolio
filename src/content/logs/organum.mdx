Egypt has maybe three pipe organs in the whole country.

You cannot walk into a local church and hear Bach played on real pipes. Mobile organ apps sound thin, and commercial VSTs cost hundreds of dollars while demanding gigabytes of sample libraries.

I built one from scratch, modeled after the **St. Johannis-Harvestehude** organ in Hamburg.

[L'Amour Toujours as played on Organum](/audio/organum.mp3)

[Organum](https://github.com/seifzellaban/organum) is a real-time pipe organ synthesizer written in Python. It computes each note live from sine waves, harmonic profiles, and room acoustics on a standard laptop without loading samples or soundfonts.

Below are the DSP bugs I hit along the way and how I fixed them.



## Why Additive Synthesis

Commercial organ VSTs record real pipes and play back the samples. That approach works, yet I skipped samples for three reasons.

First, samples lock in one recording state. A real pipe organ shifts with the room, wind pressure, and the number of pipes speaking at once.

Second, organ registrations offer near-infinite stop combinations. Recording each combination is impossible, so sample libraries mix isolated stems instead, and trained ears spot the seams right away.

Third, I wanted to learn the physics from first principles. Additive synthesis builds complex tones by summing sine waves at integer multiples of the fundamental frequency, each weighted by an amplitude. That amplitude list is the **harmonic profile**, and principal, flute, reed, and string stops each follow distinct curves. Making those sums sound like pipes instead of test tones took most of the work.



## Stop Profiles Across Pipe Families

Each pipe family has a distinct harmonic fingerprint. A principal stop carries a strong fundamental with balanced even and odd harmonics and a steady rolloff toward upper partials. A flute stays close to a pure fundamental with odd harmonics. A reed approaches a sawtooth wave with a dense harmonic series and a \( \frac{1}{k} \) amplitude rolloff.

No single textbook equation gave me realistic stop voices. Pipe organ builders have refined voicing by ear for centuries, so I compared my output against reference recordings and tuned amplitudes by ear until each family matched.

Reed stops gave me the hardest time. Real reed pipes cut through an ensemble with an aggressive edge. Reproducing that bite without sounding synthetic required changing the rolloff curve to \( \frac{1}{k} \cdot 0.92^k \) instead of pure \( \frac{1}{k} \), paired with a realistic attack transient.



## The Chiff Problem

Pressing an organ key does not start a clean steady tone right away. Wind rushes into the pipe first, creating a short breathy burst called **chiff** before the pitch settles. Without chiff, the synth sounds sterile, like a flat electronic organ.

I modeled chiff as a short burst of filtered noise at note-on with a fast decay envelope, shaped per pipe family: brighter for principals, breathier for flutes, and buzzier for reeds.

Historical tracker-action organs also produce a **tracker click**, the low-frequency mechanical thump of a valve opening. Without it, note onsets sound too clean.

I precomputed both transients into lookup tables mixed in at note-on. That part worked on the first try, until fast passages exposed the **machine gun problem**.



## Killing the Machine Gun Artifact

Striking the same key fast in succession spawns a fresh voice on each press. Firing chiff and tracker click on each strike turns rapid organ passages into a snare drum. Real pipes stay energized between fast repeats and skip the second onset transient.

To fix this, Organum stores the last release timestamp per note. If a key is struck again within 500 ms of its previous release, the new voice suppresses chiff and tracker click. I track time with a **sample clock**, an integer counter incremented per render block, instead of calling `time.monotonic()`. The audio callback runs on a real-time OS thread where syscalls risk buffer dropouts, whereas reading an integer counter costs nothing.



## Click-Free ADSR Envelopes

Naive envelopes click on note-on and note-off. A linear attack ramps amplitude from 0 to 1, jumping from slope \( 0 \) to a non-zero slope at \( t = 0 \). At short attack times, that kink produces an audible click.

I replaced the linear ramp with a **smoothstep** attack curve: \( 3t^2 - 2t^3 \) over \([0,1]\). Its derivative hits zero at both endpoints, so notes enter with zero slope, rise to peak, and level off with zero slope again. No click, no artificial punch.

Release uses an exponential decay, \( e^{-1.2t} \), matching how air energy drains from a real pipe: a fast initial drop followed by a long tail.



## The Retrigger Problem

Re-pressing a key while it sits in release introduced a second click.

Restarting the attack stage from 0 while the voice sits at amplitude 0.6 drops the signal from 0.6 to 0 in a single sample, causing a loud pop. Scaling smoothstep to run from 0.6 to 1.0 still fails because \( 3t^2 - 2t^3 \) preserves zero slope at its bounds, whereas an arbitrary starting slope from the decaying release creates a kink.

The fix is a dedicated **RETRIGGER** stage. On note-on during release, the voice runs a 15 ms Hermite crossfade (\( 3t^2 - 2t^3 \) used as a blend weight) from its current amplitude to sustain level, then enters sustain while keeping oscillator phase intact. Amplitude, slope, and phase all stay continuous across the seam.



## The Normalization Pop

Summing multiple active voices clips without gain scaling. Equal-power mixing scales output by \( \frac{1}{\sqrt{N}} \), where \( N \) is the active voice count.

Applying that scale as a constant per block creates a discrete jump whenever \( N \) changes. Stepping from 1 voice to 2 drops gain from 1.0 to 0.707 in a single sample, producing a 30 percent amplitude pop on new notes.

Organum avoids this by ramping gain in a straight line from the previous value to the new target across the full render block. A 4096-sample block at 44100 Hz spans about 93 ms, long enough to hide the gain slide while still tracking chords in real time.



## Schroeder Reverb Architecture

An organ needs a large stone room to sound complete. Small-room and spring reverbs feel wrong; cathedral acoustics demand long decay, dense late reflections, and low-end stone resonance.

I built a **Schroeder reverb** network with:

- An 18 to 48 ms pre-delay buffer separating dry sound from first reflections
- Seven parallel comb filters modeling distinct room reflections
- Four series allpass filters diffusing reflections into a dense tail

Stereo output runs two independent networks with prime-offset delay lengths so left and right channels stay wide instead of collapsing to mono. Three tuned sub-bass sine oscillators fed by the dry signal with slow attack and long release add low-frequency room resonance.



## Hitting the Real-Time Budget in Python

Python is a hostile environment for real-time audio. NumPy handles the math fast enough if you eliminate allocations in the hot path.

The callback fires at 93 ms intervals and must return 4096 samples on time to prevent dropouts. My first version allocated fresh NumPy arrays per harmonic, per voice, per callback, totaling over 1,000 allocations per block at 8 voices. Python's garbage collector paused mid-callback and caused crackles.

Moving to **preallocated work buffers** created once at startup eliminated hot-path allocations. Computing shared harmonic amplitudes once per block instead of once per voice cut redundant math by 8x. After both fixes, an 8-voice render dropped to ~45 ms, staying around 49 percent of the 93 ms callback budget.



## Takeaways

Building Organum taught me more signal processing than any course because each bug made noise right through my headphones. Acoustic instruments also carry centuries of human voicing craft that physics equations alone leave out, and tuning those curves by ear was the hardest part of the project.

If you live in Egypt and want to play a cathedral organ without booking a flight to Europe, you can run it on your laptop.
